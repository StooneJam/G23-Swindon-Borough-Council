1. INTRODUCTION
1. This Performance and Learning Review (PLR) summarizes progress under the World Bank
Group Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for the Uganda for FY16-21 discussed by the Board
of Executive Directors on April 21, 2016 (Report No. 101173-UG). Preparation of the PLR began in
FY19 as intended, but was delayed due to changes in World Bank country management, a protracted
process for reaching agreement with Government on new development priorities to be supported
by the World Bank, and challenges posed by a series of unfolding socio-economic crises to which
the WBG has responded, most recently, the Desert Locust invasion and Coronavirus Disease 2019
(COVID-19) pandemic. Given the timing and the significant changes in context, this PLR serves to: (i)
provide an updated assessment of the performance and status of the CPF program; (ii) extend the CPF
period by one year; and (iii) reflect adjustments made to ensure the program’s continued relevance
and effectiveness as Uganda confronts multiple crises and embraces opportunities for a stronger
recovery.
2. The CPF was designed to contribute towards the Government of Uganda’s (GoU) vision of a country
transformed into a modern and prosperous nation by 2040. Underpinned by the 2015 Systematic
Country Diagnostic (SCD) and aligned with national priorities articulated in Uganda’s second National
Development Program (FY15-19), the CPF focused selectively on six thematic objectives grouped
among the following three strategic areas: (i) strengthening governance, accountability and service
delivery; (ii) raising incomes in rural areas; and (iii) boosting inclusive growth in urban areas.
3. Changes to the development context, coupled with implementation challenges and emerging
priorities and crises, have required substantive adjustments to the country program during the
CPF period. Safeguards concerns and evolving client demands combined to shrink the space in which
the program could operate. At the same time, challenging governance arrangements, a massive
inflow of refugees, and a series of crises, including the 2019 Desert Locust invasion and the COVID-19
pandemic, created opportunities for Uganda’s needs and Government’s priorities to align with WBG
comparative advantages, including the ability to exercise judicious flexibility and to respond rapidly.
With project cancelations, extensions and additions, the size and composition of the World Bank’s
portfolio differ significantly from that laid out in the CPF program. However, the existing CPF focus
areas and objectives remained and continue to be relevant for Uganda through the extended program
period.
4. Despite the challenging context and complex operating environment, net commitments have
increased significantly, portfolio performance is improving, and positive results are being realized
across all focus areas, as well as in enhancing social risk management. The program is building the
resilience of poor and vulnerable households, particularly in Northern Uganda; responding to refugee
inflows; increasing access to skilled health care; significantly raising agricultural yields, increasing rural
land registrations and tenure security; enhancing economic management; improving the business
environment and beginning to restore the adequacy and equity of delivery of health and education
services. Seven of twelve outcomes are on track to be achieved during the CPF period.

5. The COVID-19 pandemic threatens the health and well-being of the Ugandan people, the
country’s economy, and its development gains. Uganda recorded its first confirmed coronavirus case
on March 27, 2020 and by March 25, 2021 the number of cases totaled 40,734 and 334 fatalities.1 The
Government enforced early and stringent measures to contain the pandemic, imposing restrictions on
cross-border travel, public and private transport and public gatherings, and closing schools and other
non-essential institutions. Real Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth is projected to have fallen more
than three percentage points between FY19 and FY20, and the fiscal deficit to have worsened from 4.9
percent of GDP pre-COVID to 7.2 percent in FY20 and to about 8 percent in FY21.
6. The PLR extends the current CPF by one year to FY22, in line with the WBG COVID-19 Crisis
Response Approach Paper’s 2020 (Approach Paper) recommendation to defer the development of
new CPFs until the return of more steady conditions. This extension would allow the next CPF for a
mid-term period beyond FY23 to be more fully articulated during FY22, following presidential elections
on January 14, 2021 and in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic. The new CPF would be informed by
an upcoming Systematic Country Diagnostic update (to be completed in FY21) and to incorporate the
results of a new Uganda National Household Survey (FY21).
7. This PLR adjusts the country program to respond to COVID-19 challenges, which aligns with
ongoing deepening WBG support for human capital development, economic inclusion and social and
physical resilience and supports IDA19 commitments. Through flexible and innovative approaches,
the World Bank has found opportunities to support an expansion of human capital investments in
Uganda. The Uganda portfolio is also gradually being adjusted toward a more transformational impact
on the twin goals, increasingly utilizing performance-based financing for service delivery, and scaling
interventions that have demonstrated impact by growing the average size of operations. In response to
the series of challenges over the CPF period, the program is also focusing on improving both physical
resilience, through land management and urban development, and social resilience with better social
risk management, expanded social protection, a multi-sectoral response to forced displacement,
and renewed attention to women’s empowerment. Finally, the country program has broadened the
engagement on governance through enhanced analysis and capacity building, trust funds that include
development partners in policy dialogue, recent policy-based financing, and a significantly enhanced
community and stakeholder engagement strategy which together have allowed the reestablishment
of a reform agenda for Uganda. The results framework has been amended to reflect recent events and
expected achievements, address measurement challenges, and identify data sources.

2. MAIN CHANGES IN COUNTRY CONTEXT
A. POLITICAL DEVELOPMENTS AND OUTLOOK
8. The Government of Uganda’s development strategy remains focused on achieving middle-
income status through speeding industrialization and strengthening competitiveness, supported
largely by infrastructure investments. The mid-term review of Uganda’s second National
Development Plan (NDPII) in FY17 found that the achievement of middle-income status in 2020
would be delayed as the strategy to frontload public infrastructure investments had not yet delivered
intended results. Nevertheless, Government’s medium-term priorities remain focused on economic
transformation through infrastructure improvements. The third NDP (NDPIII, 2021-2025) aims to
strengthen competitiveness by prioritizing agriculture, mineral development, and tourism, leveraging
urbanization as a catalyst for growth, and calls for investments in energy, transport, irrigation, and
digital infrastructure.
9. The WBG country program has found practical solutions to balance the Government’s preference
for infrastructure investments with the World Bank’s emphasis on human capital, yielding good
results. An increase in infrastructure spending between 2011 and 2013 led to a decline in allocations
to the social sectors2. The Government, supported by the World Bank’s analytical and operational
efforts, in 2017 embarked on reforms to reverse this trend, increasing fiscal transfers for non-wage
recurrent and capital spending in health and education. The recurring crises facing Uganda during the
CPF period and WBG-supported responses have resulted in a new objective in the NDPIII to upskill the
Ugandan labor force and to plan for refugee inflows more effectively.
10. The general elections, held early 2021, has posed some new risks to the delivery of the World
Bank’s remaining CPF program. During the election period, heightened political activity amidst a
COVID-19 environment saw increased spending pressures, some reprioritization of the budgetary
resources away from development priorities. Since the elections, Uganda continues to experience
a somewhat charged social and security context, which if protracted could affect new pipeline and
implementation of ongoing programs. After a pause in the third quarter of FY21, six operations are
scheduled for delivery in the fourth quarter and another six operations in FY22.

contraction between 2011 to 2017, owing to droughts, sub-regional conflicts3, private sector credit
constraints, and weak execution of public projects. Adjusted GDP estimates4 show that real GDP grew
by 6.8 percent in FY19 driven by strong domestic consumption, sustained levels of public and private

13. The COVID-19 crisis has created significant fiscal and external imbalances (see Annex 4 for
key macroeconomic indicators). The expected revenue loss from COVID-19 measures to support
the private sector was estimated at 0.18 percent of GDP in FY20, while tax revenues more broadly
were expected to fall by 0.7 percentage points of GDP to 11.6 percent of GDP for FY20. The fiscal
deficit is estimated to have risen to 7.2 percent of GDP in FY20 (relative to a pre-COVID estimate of
5 percent of GDP) and to grow to about 8 percent of GDP in FY21. The fiscal deficit continues to be
funded mostly through external financing, but domestic borrowing also intensified in FY19 and FY20.
During FY20, multilateral concessional financing and commercial borrowing totaled US$670 million,
while domestic borrowing that had doubled in FY19, further expanded in FY20 to 2.8 percent of GDP.
These elevated levels of domestic borrowing at rising interest rates are crowding out private sector
borrowing. Although inflationary pressures remain subdued to date, spillover effects onto domestic
prices are expected.
14. On the external side, the collapse in consumption and investment due to COVID-19 reduced
imports and incomes earned by foreign investors and narrowed the current account deficit to an
estimated 5.9 percent of GDP in FY20 from 6.8 percent (the year before). The financing of the current
account deficit also shifted from non-debt creating FDI inflows to government borrowing. Net FDI
declined by 21 percent during FY20, while net portfolio outflows widened by an additional 0.9 percent
of GDP. To close the gap, Government borrowed about US$1.5 billion, pushing net borrowing to 4.6
percent of GDP. These inflows financed 82 percent of the external shortfall in FY20 allowing foreign
reserves to grow to 5.4 months of imports and hold at comfortable levels since June 2020 which has
stabilized the Ugandan shilling.

15. Uganda’s risk of debt distress (assessed in the April 2020 joint International Monetary Fund (IMF)-
World Bank debt sustainability analysis6) remained low, but now appears likely to deteriorate to a
moderate risk. Ugandan authorities have a good track record of sound macroeconomic management
and have committed to maintaining macroeconomic stability going forward. However, with total debt
service (interest and principal due) expected to average around 55 percent of government revenues
over the next three years, Uganda faces heightened liquidity vulnerabilities. Maintaining public debt
on a sustainable path will require raising tax revenues; strengthening the budget process to ensure
that budget targets become more binding; improving the effectiveness of public spending and public
debt management (including continuing to maximize concessional borrowing); and undertaking more
comprehensive monitoring, controlling and reporting of fiscal risks (including contingent liabilities
and state-owned enterprise debt). With heightened risks and the country’s reduced capacity to carry
debt, the risk of debt distress is expected to deteriorate to moderate in the next Debt Sustainability
Analysis (DSA).
C. CHANGES IN POVERTY REDUCTION AND SHARED PROSPERITY.
16. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, Uganda’s progress on poverty reduction and shared prosperity
was mixed and characterized by high vulnerability to weather shocks.7 Poverty in Uganda,
measured by the national poverty line, declined from about 39 percent in 2002/03 to about 20 percent
in 2012/13. According to the latest available official poverty estimates, the poverty rate increased
modestly from 19.7 in 2012/13 to 21.4 percent in 2016/17, resulting in about 1.4 million Ugandans
slipping into poverty. A severe drought in 2016 and 2017 which negatively affected the incomes of
agricultural households was the most important contributing factor to the poverty increase which
was most pronounced in rural areas. Between 2012 and 2016, the bottom 40 percent of the population
also benefited less from economic gains, resulting in a rise in inequality. Vulnerability remained high
with two out of three people falling back into poverty due to income fluctuations, food insecurity, and
climate-related shocks. Given high economic growth in 2018 and 2019, Uganda might have returned
to a steady pace of consumption growth and poverty reduction during this period.
17. In the last decade, Uganda has seen minimal progress in the Human Capital Index (HCI). The
HCI rose from 0.34 in 2010 to 0.388 in 2020 (pre-COVID, mainly measured in 2018) reflecting modest
increases in all the composite indicators, including under 5 mortality and nutrition, years of schooling,
learning outcomes, and life expectancy.

protection programs have not proven adequate. After restrictions were put in place in March 2020,
about 17 percent of respondents to the June 2020 round of the Uganda High Frequency Phone Survey

D. EMERGING DEVELOPMENT CHALLENGES AND OPPORTUNITIES
19. The CPF period has seen an increasing centralization of decision-making which has muted the
effectiveness of Uganda’s historically strong development institutions. Political considerations are
increasingly overriding technical analysis in a broader range of development issues – and beyond
electoral periods – often leading to delays in program design and implementation. Uganda’s
Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) on voice and accountability, government effectiveness and
regulatory quality have trended downward from 2013 to 2018. Reversing an earlier decline between
2008 and 2013, the control of corruption indicator has seen a marginal increase in the last five years
reflecting the creation of an anti-corruption unit in the State House in 2018 and an anti-corruption
division in the High Court.
20. The CPF period has been marked by safeguard issues requiring strong responses by the
World Bank and the Government. Four requests for inspection cases have been initiated for
Uganda operations – the most notable in December 2015 involving gender-based violence (GBV)/
sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) and labor influx issues under the Transport Sector Development
Project (TSDP - P092837). The World Bank canceled the project, suspended the roads components
of two additional projects, and froze the Uganda pipeline in FY17. Implementation of the resulting
Management Action Plan has led to a significant strengthening of the National Roads Authority’s
capacity for social risk management (now sharing its experience elsewhere in Africa), improved
collaboration among government agencies, a Government pilot of new methods in the identification
and management of GBV cases, the inclusion of strengthened safeguards measures in civil works
bidding documents, and the retrofitting of 16 projects in the Uganda portfolio with improved GBV/SEA
measures. Three subsequent operations are notable: (i) the Development Response to Displacement
Impacts Project (DRDIP, P152822) fosters social and cultural change toward gender equality and
enhancing district capacity for prevention and response to GBV both in refugee and hosting
communities; (ii) the Supporting Children’s Opportunities through Protection and Empowerment
(SCOPE, P161704) has helped to improve response and prevention of sexual violence against children
(VAC); and (iii) the COVID-19 Economic Crisis and Recovery Development Policy Financing (P173906)
has supported approval of the National Child Policy to address violence against children and young

being restructured now, will benefit over 2 million direct and indirect beneficiaries through livelihood
recovery and further strengthening early warning systems.

3. SUMMARY OF PROGRAM IMPLEMENTATION
A. PROGRAM AND PORTFOLIO PERFORMANCE
24. Aligned with Government’s priorities from NPDII and seeking to address key constraints to
the twin goals, the CPF lays out three main areas of engagement: (i) strengthening governance,
accountability and service delivery; (ii) raising income in rural areas; and (iii) boosting inclusive growth
in urban areas which remain relevant in the COVID-19 and post COVID-19 periods. The financing
program included a large, ongoing, but young portfolio in FY16, and proposed an indicative lending
program with identified operations for the first two years (FY16 and FY17) and areas for potential future
lending for the remaining four years (FY18-FY21).
25. The size and composition of the program differ markedly from that laid out in the CPF due to
changes in context and other developments and the World Bank’s responsiveness (see Annex 5).
The resumption of budget support envisioned in FY16 was stymied by governance challenges. After
two failed attempts to deliver, the five planned or indicative DPOs referenced in the CPF were dropped
due to concerns shared by all stakeholders. Then, the macroeconomic, fiscal, and social imperatives
brought on by COVID-19 raised the exceptional need for an emergency budget support operation which
was approved in June 2020, following extensive discussions with Uganda’s development partners and
WBG shareholders. Delays in project effectiveness (averaging 14 months) and other implementation
challenges required ten extensions of project closing dates. The pipeline was suspended in FY17
because of safeguard issues described above (para. 19) and poor portfolio performance. The World
Bank acted quickly and effectively during FY19-21 to help Uganda respond to refugee inflows
with US$500 million in additional IDA financing, and in FY20 to the Desert Locust invasion and the
COVID-19 pandemic with four unanticipated operations. Provision of health, education and several
other local services shifted from line ministries to local governments, creating opportunities for new
engagements with local governments that have helped to avoid some common bottlenecks at the
central government level. The development objectives of recently delivered and planned projects
respond directly to IDA19 priorities (see Annex 5).

refugee windows and Locust12 and COVID-19 financing, while the average size of operations has

33. The World Bank plays a leading role in promoting development effectiveness in Uganda as co-

Commonwealth and Development Office, United States Agenda for International Development
and the European Union. The LDPG has revived the partnership dialogue on budget processes and

34. Trust funds catalyze collaboration among development partners. The World Bank’s portfolio
currently includes 83 active global trust fund grants totaling US$148 million with the majority (80

Trust Fund (MDTF – TF073022) to help Uganda achieve a sustainable exit from dependency on aid.
Since 2018, the MDTF has attracted more partners, mobilized additional funds, and expanded areas of
support, including in: (i) economic governance and fiscal management; (ii) public sector service delivery
and constraints to private sector participation; (ii) management of natural and tourism resources,

and job creation. This partnership is an increasingly important vehicle for: (i) coordinating strategic
responses to national development priorities and thus lowering Government’s transaction costs; (ii)
deeper donor collaboration; (iii) building the knowledge and evidence base; and more recently, (iv)
addressing the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic.

stakeholders’ views on the WBG are positive, that WBG work helps Uganda achieve development
results, and its development priority areas are aligned with what they believe the WBG should focus
its resources. Topping the list of priorities is agriculture, health, education, corruption, and jobs, while
capacity development, WBG’s support to institutional capacity got high level of emphasis.

C. OVERVIEW OF PROGRESS TOWARDS ACHIEVING CPF OBJECTIVES
36. The CPF was appropriately selective with three focus areas, six objectives and two objective-
level indicators for each objective. Thematic objectives were pitched higher than project outputs and
outcomes as guidance advises, but lower than Uganda’s development goals, and with measurements
to be drawn from national surveys and databases20. The assessment below considers the achievement
of targets in the original results framework and relevant results from additional operations, activities,
and indicators.
37. Important results had been achieved in all outcome areas. Multiple challenges have impacted
implementation of the CPF requiring the WBG to make frequent adjustments and leading to slower
progress than intended towards meeting the program’s objectives. Seven of the twelve objective
indicators are on track, two are off track, and three indicators are unable to be assessed. Of the 31
supplemental indicators, 14 have already been achieved, eight are on track, and nine are off track or
unable to be assessed. (See Annex 3 for detailed Results Monitoring Matrix).

Objective 1: Enhanced economic governance and fiscal management
38. Prior to the COVID-19 outbreak, the revenue mobilization objective indicator (OI) was on track
while the equity of spending outcome indicator remained off track21, but there has been recent and

of 3 percent. In terms of equity of spending, the ratio of average general spending per capita between
the ten best-funded and the ten worst-funded districts has shown progress, but neither the health
nor the education-related indicator related to equity is currently on track due to the cancellation of
the planned DPO series and two-year effectiveness delay of Intergovernmental Fiscal Transfer Project

equity of financial service delivery, helping to accelerate progress. In 2019/20, budgeted operational
funding for schools increased by 25 percent and for health facilities by 43 percent. Improved equitable
formulae for conditional non-wage recurrent and conditional development grants in health and
education are now being implemented as planned. Average conditional non-wage recurrent grants for

(OI2) to capture the critical issue of adequacy of funding for LG services. The original indicator
measured equality as distinct from equity.
39. Public financial transparency has improved significantly, but there is need for more progress
on budget execution. Improvements in the credibility of revenue estimates and payroll function are
noteworthy. The Ministry of Finance now discloses budget execution data for all MDAs, exceeding a target

Procurement Portal meets open data standards with more MDAs uploading their procurement
data and using the shared public service delivery platform. MoFPED has continued to consistently
publish data on total public debt, guarantees, Public Private Partnership (PPPs), contingent liabilities,
including from legal proceedings, and domestic arrears. Representing a significant increase, audits
now cover almost 37 percent of the value of the national procurement budget and the Bank of Uganda
reports on the implementation of external audit recommendations. The percentage of projects in the
public investment program underpinned by cost benefit analysis increased from 10 percent (2015)
to almost 46 percent (2020) against a target of 60 percent (2021). However, the average deviation of
the actual MDAs spending from approved budgets has worsened due to procurement-related delays
and stricter safeguard arrangements for Bank-financed projects, and there has been a deterioration in
control of payment arrears in tax audits and reconciliation of assets. The approved Public Investment

the MDTF aims to further strengthen management and financing of public investments and introduce

Objective 2: Improved social service delivery
40. With gains realized since 2019, the objective indicator related to education is now on track. After
a decade of stagnation, primary school completion rates in Uganda have recently shown improvement.
The lower secondary school gross enrollment rate increased from 37 percent in 2015 to 42 percent
in 2019. Teacher absenteeism rates – a key determinant of learning – in schools participating in the
World Bank-supported Uganda Teacher and School Effectiveness Project (TSEP, P133780) declined
from 27 percent in 2017 to 9 in 2019, exceeding the target of 10 percent. The Secondary Education
Expansion Project (P166570), currently awaiting effectiveness, aims to enhance equitable access
to lower secondary education in underserved areas and for underserved populations. The World
Bank-managed Global Partnership for Education COVID-19 Emergency Education Response Project is
poised to support continuity of learning and school re-openings.
41. Prior to the COVID-19 outbreak, the health-related objective indicator supported by a series of
World Bank-financed investment operations was on track. Deliveries with skilled attendants, targeted
under the ongoing URMCHIP operation (P155186) and the previous Health Systems Strengthening
(P115563) and Reproductive Health Voucher (P144102) projects, grew from 50 percent in 2015 to 63 in
2019 and is on track to achieve the target of 65 by the end of the CPF period. Between 2012 and 2016,
maternal mortality rates declined from 395 deaths per 100,000 live births to 343. The proportion of
qualified health workers as measured by “approved posts in public facilities filled by qualified health
workers” increased from 56 percent in FY11 to 75 percent in FY19, slightly below the FY20 Health Sector
Development Plan target of 80 percent. The percentage of facilities without stock outs of six tracer
drugs improved from 64 percent in 2015 to 84 percent in 2019, surpassing the FY20 project target of
76 percent. The East Africa Public Health Laboratories Network Project (EAPHLN P153665) has made
excellent progress in strengthening laboratory systems in Uganda, with four satellite laboratories
attaining the gold standard ISO 15189 accreditation and another four certified by the African Society
for Laboratory Medicine (ASLM), surpassing project targets. These accredited and ASLM certified
laboratories are well-positioned to support quality assured COVID-19 testing. This regional project
also supported the establishment of the Uganda Tuberculosis Supranational Laboratory (SRL) that
serves as a regional and global center of excellence providing technical support to other countries in
the region.
42. However, the COVID-19 pandemic and the re-directed focus of the health sector to fight COVID-19
may be putting these achievements at risk. The COVID-19 response has resulted in shortfalls in key
health care resources including wages, medicines and supplies, and operational funds. The situation
is complicated further by demand side factors such as fear of clients to visit health facilities, reduced
household incomes to pay user fees at private facilities, and inadequate information regarding changes
in service delivery following adjustments made to accommodate the COVID-response. Between April
and June 2020, the coverage of antenatal care, in-facility deliveries, and some immunization services
dropped by between 5 and 30 percent compared to the same period in FY19. Similar trends have been
seen in malaria, Tuberculosis, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) care, as well as for chronic
care services. The number of maternal deaths per 100,000 health facility deliveries increased by 7.6
percent, 92 per 100,000 in FY19 to 99 per 100,000 in FY20 (MoH, 2020).

Objective 3: Increased agricultural commercialization
43. Targeted yields for most crops were achieved in 2018 and the growth in income of project
beneficiaries, including women participants, has exceeded targets. The Agricultural Technology
and Advisory Services Project (ATAAS, P109224) contributed to substantial increases in the yields of
maize, rice, cassava and beans in participating areas. Targets for beans, maize and rice were exceeded,
while the increase in cassava yields is on track to meet the target in 2021. Net agricultural incomes
of project beneficiaries reportedly grew over 200 percent, significantly exceeding targets, and were
higher than those of non-beneficiaries growing maize (by 58 percent), rice (by 40 percent), cassava (by
28 percent), and beans (by 400 percent). Over half of the of beneficiaries (52 percent) under the ATAAS
project were women. Over 94 percent of these reported stronger empowerment in farmers groups;
and more than half of participating households reported joint decision making on the purchase, sale
and utilization of assets. After facing a long effectiveness delay up to January 2018, the Agricultural
Cluster Development Project (ACDP, P145037) reported a 10 percent increase in the production
of crops in participating areas, accelerated profiling and registration for e-vouchers from 87,272 to
180,000 farmers from FY19 to FY20, exceeding the target of 100,000 (FY21), and a 120 percent increase
in the number of farmers accessing inputs. Among participating farmers, marketed volumes of maize,
cassava and coffee are increasing towards targets. National level data, however, is not available. The
revised results framework thus substitutes the original Objective Indicator 5 with an indicator that
measures yield increases directly attributable to the project.
44. A WBG effort to support irrigation and commercialization of farming in Uganda is underway. The
Government is currently preparing a comprehensive irrigation masterplan while the ACDP supports the
design of new public irrigation schemes focused on medium-scale rice production. The new Irrigation
for Climate Resilience Project (ICDP, P163836) will support the development of sustainable irrigation
schemes. The IFC is helping to increase private sector participation in irrigated agriculture as well as
in value chain development and access to finance for smallholders, cooperatives and farmer groups.
The IFC also supported the design of a pilot for farmer-led and micro-scale development to attract
private capital, and a sustainable modality combining a subsidy scheme, farmers contributions, and
credit to crowd in private sector investors and scale the impact on beneficiaries.
45. The World Bank is also contributing to improving land management. The Uganda Economic
Update #6 (2015) recommended accelerating systematic land registration and strengthening institutions
for land administration management; and the Competitiveness and Enterprise Development Project
(P130471) piloted the systematic registration of communal and individually owned land. The Uganda
Support to Municipal Infrastructure Development Project Additional Financing (USMID-AF, P117876) is
focused on strengthening land tenure security in refugee hosting districts. The percentage of rural land
registered increased from 5 percent in 2014 to 10.8 percent in 2019, exceeding the 10 percent target
(2021), although the indicator cannot yet be tracked by gender.

D. EMERGING LESSONS
51. The following key lessons emerging from implementation experience have informed the
proposed adjustments to the current CPF and will help to shape the preparation of the new CPF:
52. Investing in social risk management pays off in better and more sustainable outcomes. As
described above (para 20), the CPF period was marked by safeguard-related challenges. Inspection
Panel requests and subsequent investigations documented weaknesses in managing social risks
in large infrastructure projects and in contract management. These findings have led to a greater
understanding of broader social risks in Uganda, strengthened safeguards management by both
Government and the World Bank, and made stronger and more systematic the focus on compliance.
In addition, the World Bank is increasingly promoting culturally sensitive approaches to difficult social
inclusions issues and has advanced from a “do no harm” perspective to enhancing environmental and
social results in project design. Social risk management has also provided an additional entry point
for dialogue on women’s empowerment and girls’ development, including for refugee households
who are particularly vulnerable.
53. Flexible, creative, and practical responses can help to bridge challenging political dynamics and
deliver results. The WBG relationship in Uganda has been challenged by the authorities’ preference
to channel development finance more toward infrastructure than social service delivery, increasingly
centralized decision-making, and emerging weaknesses in governance (para 7). However, innovative
solutions have helped to close the gaps and deliver results without damaging the client- World Bank
relationship. These innovative approaches, which will be continued, include:
• Broadening partnerships across government entities, developing institutional capacity of local
agencies, enhancing citizen engagement through targeted stakeholder consultations, and deeper
collaboration with development partners which have combined to build strong coalitions focused
on service delivery.
• Financing health and education infrastructure and services through results-based approaches
that strengthen the link between performance and financing aim to address the adequacy of local

5. RISKS TO THE CPF PROGRAM
63. The overall risk27 to the achievement of CPF goals remains substantial despite strong results
to date and the short period remaining, due to the extreme uncertainties posed by the COVID-19
pandemic on the economic and social outcomes, and by the heightened social tensions in
Uganda. The macroeconomic risk has been raised from moderate to High; risks related to the
political and governance, sector policies, institutional capacity, fiduciary management, and social
and environmental issues remain substantial or high. Relevant targets in the results framework have
been recalibrated to include a likely slowdown in key development gains impacted by COVID-19, but
significant uncertainties remain.
64. Political and governance risks to the program remain Substantial. Heightened political activity
in the run up to 2021 elections diverted attention from development challenges, which may persist if
social tensions are protracted. The demands of the COVID-19 response have decreased discretionary
fiscal space while drawing increased public scrutiny to the details of crisis spending. The CPF program
avoided programming new operations during and immediately following the election. To mitigate
broader and persistent governance challenges, including increasing centralization of decision-making
and corruption, the CPF program will continue to help deepen technical capacities in central and local
government, enhance citizen engagement to strengthen accountability, and support digitalization of
government services.
65. Macroeconomic risks to the CPF program have been upgraded to High. Although Uganda’s
growth projections remain positive, there is considerable uncertainty ahead. Uganda has a record of
sound macroeconomic policy adjustments, but there are downside risks of a protracted pandemic or
global recession, further restrictions that disrupt domestic activity, and fiscal slippages. The upcoming
(FY21) debt sustainability analysis could raise Uganda’s risk of debt distress. Efforts to safeguard fiscal
sustainability will needed to mitigate these risks. The IMF has recently reengaged in Uganda with the
delivery of a COVID-19 Rapid Response Facility in May 2020, while World Bank continues to support
improvements in the governance of tax expenditures, local government revenue mobilization, and the
efficiency of revenue administration.
66. The risk related to sector strategies and policies reduced to Moderate. Building on progress
already achieved, the CPF continues to aim at expanding the Uganda’s programming toward core IDA
priorities, including gender equity, social inclusion, and human capital. Policy dialogue, innovative
programming (and emerging results), and strategic ASA are helping to find areas of agreement,
including those that have emerged during recent crises.
67. The risk of institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability is Moderate. To mitigate
the impact of Uganda’s capacity deficits, institutional weaknesses, and highly centralized decision-

Strategic Focus Area A: Strengthening governance, accountability, and service delivery
All CPF objectives complement proposed improvements in social service delivery and improved governance, management, and accountability for public resources, which will
accelerate poverty reduction and boost shared prosperity. While improvements in health and education are important, migrants to urban areas need strong human capital
development to be able to access better jobs that will be created over the medium term. Good health and education would also empower women to access opportunity in
a growing Ugandan economy. Increasing economic growth and tax revenue while efficiently allocating and spending resources will maximize the poverty impact and boost
shared prosperity.
CPF Objective 1: Enhanced economic governance and fiscal management
Intervention Logic: The public sector should become more effective in using public resources by closing the policy implementation gap to help Uganda accelerate poverty
reduction. This requires four primary changes: (i) increasing revenues to expand the fiscal space to finance infrastructure and social service needs; (ii) improving the allocation
of resources both between sectors and levels of government; (iii) preparing for the effective management of future oil revenues and improving debt management; (iv)
enhancing expenditure management through improved PFM; and (v) improving efficiency of public investment management, including procurement management. This needs
to be complemented by strengthening country systems for transparency, participation. This objective contributes to Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 8, 10, and 16.
Therefore, the WBG will support Uganda through a series of interventions and ASAs to enhance revenue at the central and the sub-national level, including efficiency of
resource allocation, using poverty maps and analysis to target the poor. This will be supported by lending operations with strong sector content that will seek to improve
resource allocation for service delivery to local governments and enhance transparency and accountability. In addition, an investment project will focus on statistical capacity
building to close systematic gaps as well as those within the priority areas covered by the CPF. On enhancing expenditure management, the World Bank will design a results-
based operation aimed at improving the use of public financial and procurement management systems; making budget planning and execution more transparent, reducing
procurement delays while enhancing integrity respectively; and improving the sanctions regime. The operation will complement reforms to enhance citizen engagement using
social accountability mechanisms. The WBG will continue the technical assistance to the implementation of the debt management strategy; and continue the mandatory debt
sustainability assessments jointly with the IMF, especially in the context of future oil flows.
Challenges: While support for formal changes in policies has been strong in recent years, enthusiasm has flagged during the implementation phase. There is also a risk
that vested interests of different stakeholder’s delay reforms, and that the government will continue to find it difficult to translate important policy reforms into practical
implementation.

CPF Objective 2: Improved social service delivery
Intervention Logic: Much of Uganda’s inclusive development in the short and longer terms critically depends on helping people develop capacities to become self-reliant and
productive participants in Uganda society. The Human capital Index for Uganda indicates a child born in Uganda today will only be 38 percent as productive as she could be if
she had completed education and maintained full health when growing up; which ranks the country 137 out of 157 on the HCI. To seize opportunities people must have a basic
level of health, knowledge, and skills. The Government’s primary role in this process is to provide the foundations of primary healthcare services and basic education. Health
services would include services that address the triple burden of reproductive maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health conditions and malnutrition; infectious diseases
such as malaria; and non-communicable disease that are becoming prevalent. Basic education services would focus on providing children and adults a full cycle of education
with skills and cognitive development to tap into labor market opportunities. Addressing under-nutrition would help to improve health and learning outcomes, and enhance
productivity thereby contributing to sustained and equitable economic growth.
In primary education, access is nearly universal, but quality is variable and many students, particularly from poorer households, dropout before completion. Access to
secondary education is limited, and financing remains inefficient. These shortcomings are particularly prominent in the North and East of the country. With the closures of
schools due to COVID-19 the inequalities in access to education have widened further and will have an impact on the learning outcomes. The World Bank will continue to
implement its investment in improving quality and equity of primary education by, among others, improving teacher effectiveness, school level accountability systems, and
focusing on schools in rural areas, including measures to prevent sexual and gender-based violence (a common cause of school dropouts amongst girls) against children
both in and out of schools. A new project will focus on increasing access to and quality of secondary education including refugee children. Of importance is progression and
retention of girls in secondary school to expand their education and labor market opportunities and indirectly slow population growth. Finally, the World Bank will continue
ongoing work in skills development by supporting high quality and demand driven skills training in key growth sectors (oil and gas, agriculture & construction) Given Uganda’s
young population the World Bank will likely support a new operation on youth employment to help Ugandans become more employable as the labor market evolves.
The underlying challenges facing the health sector and constraining its performance arise from the high disease burden (the majority of which is preventable) and under-
resourcing, coupled with ineffective and inefficient deployment of human resources, medicines, equipment and infrastructure. Low management and operational capacity
in the cities, local governments and health facilities dampen the capacity of these entities to deliver high quality health services required to achieve universal health coverage
objectives. As a result, coverage of several key programs is suboptimal, and furthermore, there is significant regional variation in coverage and quality of care. World Bank
support Government centers on strengthening health systems and infrastructure, improving incentives for health providers, and increasing the results focus of the Government’s
health programs. Other partners will focus their efforts on disease prevention, including for specific diseases and through more general approaches including hygiene and
water. In both health and education, the World Bank will help the government support both public and private sector provision of services. This objective contributes to SDGs
3, 4, 5, and 10.
Challenges: Government financing of health and education has been declining as a share of the budget and of GDP, and given the rapidly growing population and government
policies to provide free basic health and education services, adequate resourcing of the sector is important to ensure adequate access to and quality of services. This ultimately
reflects as severe shortage of critical resourcing including staffing, infrastructure, equipment, materials/medicines and low level of motivation among service providers
(facilities and personnel), a situation that has been accentuated by proliferation of districts and high population growth rate unmatched by resource allocation.

Strategic Focus Area B: Raising incomes in rural areas
In the short to medium term, there is great opportunity in Uganda for rural development and increases in income in rural areas, which will lead directly to robust poverty
reduction. Growth in agriculture has had a strong impact on poverty in recent years. The livelihoods of more than four in five Ugandans are dependent on income from
agriculture. But compared to neighboring countries, agricultural growth has been slow, productivity is low, and entire regions of the country have largely been excluded.
Variability in income due to external shocks leaves many families vulnerable to falling back into extreme poverty even after they had initially escaped it.
CPF Objective 3: Increased agricultural commercialization
Intervention logic: Growth in the agriculture sector requires higher agricultural productivity and increased commercialization, given the limited remaining potential
for expansion of cultivated land, and the unpredictability of weather and prices. The WBG program takes a broad approach to boosting productivity and increasing
commercialization, including interventions in many sectors that will encourage farmers to invest in their land. Constraints brought on by COVID-19 are likely to impact land
area cultivated, agricultural productivity due to low production, reduce household incomes and increase the risk of food insecurity. The program will tackle access to and
security of land and access to finance, so farmers have the capacity to invest. It will support improved extension services, so farmers have the right information to invest and
to adopt sustainable farming practices using high quality agricultural inputs. The program will mitigate the impacts of climate change manifested in new pests and diseases,
case in point being the Fall Army Worm (2017) a new pest on both cereal and grain crops, as well as new diseases such Cassava Brown Streak Disease (2016), and more recently,
the Desert Locust invasion of 2020 that threatened to reverse productivity increases, all of which require investments in research for control measures. And it will help farmers
capture economies of scale, including through associations and commercialization. The program will also help farmers get better prices, creating the incentive to invest. This
will include strengthening value chain coordination in agribusiness and providing better price information and storage solutions so farmers can reduce post-harvest losses, sell
when prices are attractive, and face lower transport costs to markets. As observed by the SCD, given current income levels, the high share of income going to consumption, and
the relatively closed economy, an increase in income can produce large multiplier effects. Key indicators are both yields and marketed volumes of selected crops. These will
show both that farmers are producing more, and that they are receiving a return on their investment and labor by getting their product to market. This objective contributes
to SDGs 1, 2, 7, 8, 9, and 10.
Challenges: First, strategic and policy uncertainty combined with capacity constraints in Ministry of Agriculture, Animal Industry and Fisheries have hampered progress on
previous direct support to the agriculture sector. Second, there is a time lag between land registration, access to finance, and greater investment by farmers, suggesting
increased productivity through this lever may not begin until the end of the CPF period. Third, the agriculture sector has a track record of slow implementation. Fourth,
the dependence on rain-fed agriculture and volatile commodity prices makes yields and productivity highly sensitive to climate and external conditions. Fourth, ultimately
agricultural productivity will continue to rely heavily on weather conditions and associated pests and diseases necessitating development of early warning capabilities.

CPF Objective 4: Enhanced resilience of the poor and vulnerable
Intervention logic: Even as agricultural productivity increases, people in rural areas are increasingly vulnerable to long-term degradation of natural resources, droughts, and
volatility in farm-gate prices. Indeed, in recent years, for every three people to rise out of poverty, two have fallen back into poverty. Mitigation of shocks—by helping people who
face them, and building up household and community assets, which increase resilience to shocks - is essential to stability and to reducing vulnerability.
The World Bank will support Uganda in expanding its system of safety nets, focused in particularly on the vulnerable Northern region, to help those already in poverty and
those who have fallen back into poverty and build resilience, mitigate risks and support vulnerable households to invest in human capital especially in times of shocks such
as COVID-19 and climate-related. It will also support those communities hosting refugees displaced from conflicts in neighboring countries, and help the government protect
vulnerable groups through improved social risk management, including prevention of sexual and GBV related to infrastructure development and public works, which accounts
for a large share of WBG activities. In addition, the World Bank will support social development dialogue and client capacity building to increase inclusion, sustainability,
transparency, and accountability in development-induced land acquisition. For water for production, the World Bank will support the government’s development of integrated
water resources management plans for key catchments and invest in monitoring and information systems for sustainable water flows and water for production including
expanding the use of irrigation to mitigate against droughts.
Investments in water for production will be made late in the CPF period, so results will only become apparent during the following CPF cycle. Support to farmers will include a
shift to ‘climate smart’ agriculture. The World Bank will also support investments in the environment and natural resources, including projects in forests and climate adaptation,
to help preserve the eco-system on which Ugandan livelihoods depend. This objective contributes to SDGs 6, 13, and 15.
Challenges: Difficulties in targeting safety net support to the poorest, balancing expanded water production with water resources management, and the employability of
potential workers in rural areas. Furthermore, the agriculture value chain is currently not adequately developed to generate enough off-farm employment and the renewable
natural resource base is becoming rapidly degraded.

Strategic Focus Area C: Boosting inclusive growth in urban areas
In the medium to long term, as improvements in technology require agriculture to be less labor intensive and population growth creates fragmentation and potential conflict
over land, poverty gains from rural development will wane. Good jobs are the key to translating growth in urban areas into poverty reduction and shared prosperity. Migrants
from rural areas and higher-skilled workers alike will need new opportunities for formal employment in urban areas to continue poverty reduction, and accelerated urbanization
will be the conduit leading the rural poor to higher standard of living. Urban areas must be prepared for the influx of new residents while maintaining and upgrading key urban
services. The private sector should operate in a strong business environment to facilitate investments that lead to more and better jobs.
CPF Objective 5: Improved business environment
Intervention Logic: Most new jobs in Uganda come from MSMEs, but they face a difficult business environment that creates a drag on job creation and dis-incentivizes
formalization. At its base, improving the business environment to foster productive MSME growth and help new MSMEs enter the market, as well as encouraging larger
businesses to invest, involves maintaining macro-economic stability and easing overly burdensome regulation on business while maintaining necessary rules and policies.
But to invest, businesses need access to finance that comes with the certainty, transparency and accountability of land tenure and acquisition and resettlement policies, areas
where WBG support will be key. They need security in the knowledge that their costs will be predictable due to a reliable and affordable supply of electricity and urban mobility,
and that their outputs will find a market through regional transport links and lower barriers to trade. And they need a steady supply of healthy, educated, and skilled workers
to produce their goods and services.
The World Bank, MIGA and IFC will provide support in several of these areas: improving the coverage of land registration, expanding access to credit for MSMEs including
through use of non-land assets as collateral, expanding the grid to make power more accessible, and supporting the development of demand-driven skills training and
improvements in health and education services. The World Bank will carry through its existing commitments in inter-city roads, but not provide new support, as other capable
partners lead the dialogue there and the government has expressed greater demand in other areas. Since the development of the CPF in FY16, MIGA has issued in Uganda two
products focused on expanding access to credit: one capital optimization PRI benefitting Absa Bank’s subsidiary in Uganda, and one non-honoring guarantee for the Trade and
Development Bank (TDB, regional development bank for the COMESA region) to benefit its member countries including Uganda. Both projects should support liquidity and
access to finance in Uganda. This objective contributes to SDGs 8 and 9.
Challenges: Private investment is strongly influenced by the external environment which has been volatile in recent years. There is a time lag between infrastructure
investments, improvements in the business environment, and private investment by firms. There are political and capacity challenges to roll out major land registration
initiative in a short time frame. Finally, further delays in increasing the generation capacity may restrict affordability and reliability of electricity, which is a key constraint for
reducing the cost of doing business.

CPF Objective 6: Improved access to urban services
Intervention logic: To sustainably accelerate its urbanization and maintain and improve living standards in urban areas, Uganda needs to prepare for migration to cities and
small towns by strengthening the institutional capacity to develop implementable urban master plans to connect transport and services to land use as well as own source
revenue mobilization. In terms of improving urban services, the Bank will focus its efforts on access to water and sanitation and electricity. The World Bank also supports the
government of Uganda is strengthening the land acquisition, resettlement and rehabilitation policy. Additional support may come in the form of analytical work on public
transport options, laying the groundwork for future investments as well as technical assistance in selected areas such as strengthening capacity to deliver sanitation and
manage fecal sludge. IFC will work opportunistically to make investments in banks to assist with the growth of mortgage products to improve access to housing. This objective
contributes to SDGs 9, 11, and 16.
Challenges: Population growth in urban areas may move faster than the capacity to plan. The focus of policymakers could remain on current urban areas, without as much
concern for outlying areas that will urbanize soon. Improving urban mobility is extremely complex, requiring coordinated efforts from multiple ministries, and there is limited
demand expressed by the client for the World Bank to finance projects in this area.

Annex 3: CPF Results Monitoring Matrix
Strategic Focus Area A: Strengthening governance, accountability and service delivery
All CPF objectives complement proposed improvements in social service delivery and improved governance, management and accountability for public resources, which will
accelerate poverty reduction and boost shared prosperity. While improvements in health and education are important, migrants to urban areas need strong human capital
development to be able to access better jobs that will be created over the medium term. Good health and education would also empower women to access opportunity in
a growing Ugandan economy. Increasing economic growth and tax revenue while efficiently allocating and spending resources will maximize the poverty impact and boost
shared prosperity.
CPF Objective 1: Enhanced economic governance and fiscal management
Intervention Logic: The public sector must become more effective in using public resources by closing the policy implementation gap to help Uganda accelerate poverty
reduction. This requires four primary changes: (i) increasing revenues to expand the fiscal space to finance infrastructure and social service needs; (ii) improving the allocation
of resources both between sectors and levels of government; (iii) preparing for the effective management of future oil revenues and improving debt management; (iv)
enhancing expenditure management through improved PFM; and (v) improving efficiency of public investment management, including procurement management. This needs
to be complemented by strengthening country systems for transparency, participation. This objective contributes to SDGs 8, 10, and 16.
Therefore, the Bank will support Uganda through a series of interventions and ASAs to enhance revenue at the central and the sub-national level, including efficiency of
resource allocation, using poverty maps and analysis to target the poor. This will be supported by lending operations with strong sector content that will seek to improve
resource allocation for service delivery to local governments and enhance transparency and accountability. In addition, an investment project will focus on statistical capacity
building to close systematic gaps as well as those within the priority areas covered by the CPF. On enhancing expenditure management, the Bank will design a results-
based operation aimed at improving the use of public financial and procurement management systems; making budget planning and execution more transparent, reducing
procurement delays while enhancing integrity respectively; and improving the sanctions regime. The operation will complement reforms to enhance citizen engagement using
social accountability mechanisms. The Bank will continue the technical assistance to the implementation of the debt management strategy; and continue the mandatory debt
sustainability assessments jointly with the IMF, especially in the context of future oil flows.
Challenges: While support for formal changes in policies has been strong in recent years, enthusiasm has flagged during the implementation phase. There is also a risk
that vested interests of different stakeholder’s delay reforms, and that the government will continue to find it difficult to translate important policy reforms into practical
implementation.

education with skills and cognitive development to tap into labor market opportunities. Addressing under-nutrition would help to improve health and learning outcomes, and
enhance productivity thereby contributing to sustained and equitable economic growth.

secondary education is limited, and financing remains inefficient. These shortcomings are particularly prominent in the North and East of the country. With the closures of
schools due to COVID 19 the inequalities in access to education have widened further and will have an impact on the learning outcomes. The Bank will continue to implement
its investment in improving quality and equity of primary education by, among others, improving teacher effectiveness, school level accountability systems, and focusing on
schools in rural areas, including measures to prevent sexual and gender-based violence (a common cause of school dropouts amongst girls) against children both in and out
of schools. A new project will focus on increasing access to and quality of secondary education including refugee children. Of importance is progression and retention of girls
in secondary school to expand their education and labor market opportunities and indirectly slow population growth. Finally, the Bank will continue ongoing work in skills
development by supporting high quality and demand driven skills training in key growth sectors (oil & gas, agriculture & construction) Given Uganda’s young population the
Bank will likely introduce a new operation on youth employment to help Ugandans become more employable as the labor market evolves.
The underlying challenges facing the health sector and constraining its performance arise from the high disease burden (the majority of which is preventable) and under-
resourcing, coupled with ineffective and inefficient deployment of human resources, medicines, equipment and infrastructure. Low management and operational capacity
in the cities, local governments and health facilities dampen the capacity of these entities to deliver high quality health services required to achieve universal health coverage
objectives. As a result, coverage of several key programs is suboptimal, and furthermore, there is significant regional variation in coverage and quality of care. Bank support
centers on strengthening health systems and infrastructure, improving incentives for health providers, and increasing the results focus of the Government’s health programs.
Other partners will focus their efforts on disease prevention, including for specific diseases and through more general approaches including hygiene and water. In both health
and education, the Bank will help the government support both public and private sector provision of services. This objective contributes to SDGs 3, 4, 5, and 10.
Challenges: Government financing of health and education has been declining as a share of the budget and of GDP, and given the rapidly growing population and government
policies to provide free basic health and education services, adequate resourcing of the sector is important to ensure adequate access to and quality of services. This ultimately
reflects as severe shortage of critical resourcing including staffing, infrastructure, equipment, materials/medicines and low level of motivation among service providers
(facilities and personnel), a situation that has been accentuated by proliferation of districts and high population growth rate unmatched by resource allocation.

Strategic Focus Area B: Raising incomes in rural areas
In the short to medium term, there is great opportunity in Uganda for rural development and increases in income in rural areas, which will lead directly to robust poverty reduction.
Growth in agriculture has had a strong impact on poverty in recent years. The livelihoods of more than 4 in 5 Ugandans are dependent on income from agriculture. But compared
to neighboring countries, agricultural growth has been slow, productivity is low, and entire regions of the country have largely been excluded. Variability in income due to external
shocks leaves many families vulnerable to falling back into extreme poverty even after they had initially escaped it.
CPF Objective 3: Increased agricultural commercialization
Intervention logic: Growth in the agriculture sector requires higher agricultural productivity and increased commercialization, given the limited remaining potential
for expansion of cultivated land, and the unpredictability of weather and prices. The WBG program takes a broad approach to boosting productivity and increasing
commercialization, including interventions in many sectors that will encourage farmers to invest in their land. Constraints brought on by COVID19 are likely to impact land area
cultivated, agricultural productivity due to low production, reduce household incomes and increase the risk of food insecurity. The program will tackle access to and security
of land and access to finance, so farmers have the capacity to invest. It will support improved extension services, so farmers have the right information to invest and to adopt
sustainable farming practices using high quality agricultural inputs. The program will mitigate the impacts of climate change manifested in new pests and diseases, case in
point being the Fall Army Worm (2017) a new pest on both cereal and grain crops, as well as new diseases such Cassava Brown Streak Disease (2016), and more recently, the
Desert Locust invasion of 2020 that threatened to reverse productivity increases, all of which require investments in research for control measures. And it will help farmers
capture economies of scale, including through associations and commercialization. The program will also help farmers get better prices, creating the incentive to invest. This
will include strengthening value chain coordination in agribusiness and providing better price information and storage solutions so farmers can reduce post-harvest losses, sell
when prices are attractive, and face lower transport costs to markets. As observed by the SCD, given current income levels, the high share of income going to consumption, and
the relatively closed economy, an increase in income can produce large multiplier effects. Key indicators are both yields and marketed volumes of selected crops. These will
show both that farmers are producing more, and that they are receiving a return on their investment and labor by getting their product to market. This objective contributes
to SDGs 1, 2, 7, 8, 9, and 10.
Challenges: First, strategic and policy uncertainty combined with capacity constraints in Ministry of Agriculture, Animal Industry and Fisheries have hampered progress on
previous direct support to the agriculture sector. Second, there is a time lag between land registration, access to finance, and greater investment by farmers, suggesting
increased productivity through this lever may not begin until the end of the CPF period. Third, the agriculture sector has a track record of slow implementation. Fourth,
the dependence on rain-fed agriculture and volatile commodity prices makes yields and productivity highly sensitive to climate and external conditions. Fourth, ultimately
agricultural productivity will continue to rely heavily on weather conditions and associated pests and diseases necessitating development of early warning capabilities.

CPF Objective 4: Enhanced resilience of the poor and vulnerable
Intervention logic: Even as agricultural productivity increases, people in rural areas are increasingly vulnerable to long-term degradation of natural resources, droughts, and
volatility in farm-gate prices. Indeed, in recent years, for every three people to rise out of poverty, two have fallen back into poverty. Mitigation of shocks -by helping people who
face them, and building up household and community assets, which increase resilience to shocks - is essential to stability and to reducing vulnerability.
The Bank will support Uganda in expanding its system of safety nets, focused in particularly on the vulnerable Northern region, to help those already in poverty and those who
have fallen back into poverty and build resilience, mitigate risks and support vulnerable households to invest in human capital especially in times of shocks such as COVID19
and climate-related. It will also support those communities hosting refugees displaced from conflicts in neighboring countries, and help the government protect vulnerable
groups through improved social risk management, including prevention of sexual and gender-based violence related to infrastructure development and public works, which
accounts for a large share of WBG activities. In addition, the Bank will support social development dialogue and client capacity building to increase inclusion, sustainability,
transparency and accountability in development-induced land acquisition. For water for production, the Bank will support the government’s development of integrated water

Strategic Focus Area C: Boosting inclusive growth in urban areas
In the medium to long term, as improvements in technology require agriculture to be less labor intensive and population growth creates fragmentation and potential conflict
over land, poverty gains from rural development will wane. Good jobs are the key to translating growth in urban areas into poverty reduction and shared prosperity. Migrants
from rural areas and higher-skilled workers alike will need new opportunities for formal employment in urban areas to continue poverty reduction, and accelerated urbanization
will be the conduit leading the rural poor to higher standard of living. Urban areas must be prepared for the influx of new residents while maintaining and upgrading key urban
services. The private sector must operate in a strong business environment to facilitate investments that lead to more and better jobs.
CPF Objective 5: Improved business environment
Intervention Logic: Most new jobs in Uganda come from MSMEs, but they face a difficult business environment that creates a drag on job creation and dis-incentivizes
formalization. At its base, improving the business environment to foster productive MSME growth and help new MSMEs enter the market, as well as encouraging larger
businesses to invest, involves maintaining macro-economic stability and easing overly burdensome regulation on business while maintaining necessary rules and policies.
But to invest, businesses need access to finance that comes with the certainty, transparency and accountability of land tenure and acquisition and resettlement policies, areas
where WBG support will be key. They need security in the knowledge that their costs will be predictable due to a reliable and affordable supply of electricity and urban mobility,
and that their outputs will find a market through regional transport links and lower barriers to trade. And they need a steady supply of healthy, educated, and skilled workers
to produce their goods and services.
The Bank, MIGA and IFC will provide support in several of these areas: improving the coverage of land registration, expanding access to credit for MSMEs including through
use of non-land assets as collateral, expanding the grid to make power more accessible, and supporting the development of demand-driven skills training and improvements
in health and education services. The Bank will carry through its existing commitments in inter-city roads, but not provide new support, as other capable partners lead the
dialogue there and the government has expressed greater demand in other areas. Since the development of the CPF in FY16, MIGA has issued in Uganda two products focused
on expanding access to credit: one capital optimization PRI benefitting Absa Bank’s subsidiary in Uganda, and one non-honoring guarantee for the Trade and Development
Bank (TDB, regional development bank for the COMESA region) to benefit its member countries including Uganda. Both projects should support liquidity and access to finance
in Uganda. This objective contributes to SDGs 8 and 9.
Challenges: Private investment is strongly influenced by the external environment which has been volatile in recent years. There is a time lag between infrastructure
investments, improvements in the business environment, and private investment by firms. There are political and capacity challenges to roll out major land registration
initiative in a short time frame. Finally, further delays in increasing the generation capacity may restrict affordability and reliability of electricity, which is a key constraint for
reducing the cost of doing business.

CPF Objective 6: Improved access to urban services
Intervention logic: To sustainably accelerate its urbanization and maintain and improve living standards in urban areas, Uganda needs to prepare for migration to cities
and small towns by strengthening the institutional capacity to develop implementable urban master plans to connect transport and services to land use as well as own
source revenue mobilization. In terms of improving urban services, the Bank will focus its efforts on access to water and sanitation and electricity. The Bank also supports the
government of Uganda is strengthening the land acquisition, resettlement and rehabilitation policy. Additional support may come in the form of analytical work on public
transport options, laying the groundwork for future investments as well as technical assistance in selected areas such as strengthening capacity to deliver sanitation and
manage fecal sludge. IFC will work opportunistically to make investments in banks to assist with the growth of mortgage products to improve access to housing. This objective
contributes to SDGs 9, 11, and 16.
Challenges: Population growth in urban areas may move faster than the capacity to plan. The focus of policymakers could remain on current urban areas, without as much
concern for outlying areas that will urbanize in the near future. Improving urban mobility is extremely complex, requiring coordinated efforts from multiple ministries, and there
is limited demand expressed by the client for the Bank to finance projects in this area.

IDA 19 commitments
The Uganda portfolio is responding to IDA19 commitments. The planned portfolio under IDA19 (already
delivered and pipeline) responds to the IDA19 commitment on Jobs and Economic transformation.
The planned operations building on the ongoing portfolio are focused on enhancing growth and
development in the sectors of education, transport, health, energy, digital, urban development, and
agriculture. With agriculture as the sector employing the most people, the intervention is expected to
support enhancement of productivity and enhancing activities across the value chain. With respect
to gender and development, the key intervention aims to enhance human development in general
and economic empowerment to women. Gender responsiveness is a critical factor and cross cutting
within the entire portfolio. Deliberate efforts have been made in all interventions to address the
gender perspective. Climate change is another crosscutting intervention. The planned Climate Smart
Agriculture and Agribusiness project is planned to have interventions in the agriculture sector that
directly respond to climate change. On Governance and Institutions, the portfolio includes efforts to
strengthen the governance environment especially in the areas of public procurement and financial
management. Support for enhancing institutional capacity is also provided to the extent possible.
The support includes complementing the efforts already being made by the client. The Governance
commitment is also supported by the World Bank’s ASA portfolio. Below is a table outlining the IDA19
financed interventions as they respond to the commitments.
