HERITAGE
CITY EXPANSION THROUGH THE AGES
There is evidence of human occupation of the valley
and areas associated with the Miljacka River dating
back to the Neolithic period. Roman development
established the basis of the modern-day city.
Subsequent expansion of the city has evolved over
the following key socio-economic periods:
Ottoman Empire 1400 - 1878
Austro-Hungarian Empire 1878 – 1918
Kingdom of Yugoslavia 1918 – 1945
Socialist Federal Republic 1945 - 1992

HERITAGE
HISTORIC VALUE
The Skenderija Complex buildings have been
captured in fi lm, photography, televised
events and represented on stamps. The
recording of the massing and the signature
roof forms of the 1969 complex have been
replicated and offer a consistent and
progressive representation. The buildings
have an important location within the greater
city and have also functioned as a backdrop
to historical events. Key to the potential
restoration and development of the site
will be a careful preservation of its iconic
features. This works across all scales from
fabric and details to the massing rhythm of
any proposed new elements.

HERITAGE
SITE MORPHOLOGY: A BRIEF HISTORY
Analysis of the site history is key to informing
an understanding the current complex
and helps to establish a framework for
development proposals going forward.
Development of the Skender-Bey begun
in 1499 by Iskender bey Paša Mihaloglu,
or Skender Paša who built a tekke (Sufi
dervich monastery) ) and minaret on the left
back area now known as Skenderija. These
buildings were associated with a caravansary
on the right bank and most likely formed a
külliye (a complex of buildings associated
with Ottoman architecture and centered
on a mosque) linked by the original wooden
pedestrian Šejhanija Bridge built in the late
16th Century. It is likely the imaret functioned, 16th century - minaret and wooden footbridge
as the Ottoman term may suggest, as a soup
kitchen and place of refuge.
In 1518 Skender Paša son Mustafa beg
Skenderpašic built the fi rst domed mosque in
Bosnia & Herzegovina attached to the side of
his father’s tekke.
The Cobanija Bridge made of iron was built
to replace the original wooden bridge in
1887. This bridge is on the List of National
Monuments of Bosnia and Herzegovina
(declared).
The complex was used during the Austro-
Hungarian rule for entertainment and
events including visiting acts such as Cirkus
Platzon and after falling into disrepair was
demolished to form a series of sports fi elds.

HERITAGE
In line with the city’s ambitious expansion
during the 1950s and 60’s the Skenderija
site was developed into a new cultural
and sports complex by President Tito in
1969. Designed by Živorad Jankovic, Halid
Muhasilovica and Ognjen Malkin, and it won
the ‘BORBA’ architectural prize the year it
opened. The complex became a focus for
cultural and sporting life of the city and
supported arts and entertainment events.
Its unique sculptural forms, innovative
use of modern materials and complex
programmatic ambitions are still in evidence
today. At the time it also functioned as a
focal point and a social space for both the
community, the Don Mladih or Youth House,
and the wider national economic and social
development.
In the early 1980s the complex was 1969
extended to host the 1984 Winter Olympics
and became a node within the city fabric,
linking the civic and ceremonial spaces to
the sporting events held in the Zetra Hall
to Trebevic Mountain. The complex was
extended to include the Ledena Dvorana
ice skating rink and the large forecourt was
extended, stepping back from the road edge
to form an enhanced civic space addressing
the river.
Various additions to the complex have been
made since the Olympic development, some
more sympathetic than others. While the
complex continues to be used today and
number of structures have been damaged
and fallen into disrepair.

PREVIOUS MASTERPLAN
PREVIOUS MASTERPLAN
In 2008, a masterplan was developed for the
Skenderija Complex which identifi ed areas
for potential development as well as new
uses. The masterplan was not adopted as
some of the proposed building components,
in particular the massing, use or location
were not considered appropriate, for example,
the hotel or the location of the garage.
Feedback from a stakeholder engagement
meeting undertaken in January 2020
highlighted the need to review and revize the 2008
masterplan.

CONTEMPORARY CHALLENGES
EXISTING USES
Skenderija today is characterized by its
sports uses and a limited cultural offer. It
includes a number of retail and commercial
units that are performing poorly not only due
to the competition from surrounding shopping
centers but also because of the condition of
the built structures.
PUBLIC SPACE
The plaza of Skenderija is one of the largest
public spaces in Sarajevo. Today, it is
neglected and occupied by commercial units
with disputed legality.
USER EXPERIENCE
Experience in Skenderija is a product of
people’s relationship and perception of
space, environment and program. Despite
the site’s cultural and heritage significance
for the people of Skenderija, consultation
undertaken with a number of stakeholders
in Sarajevo has indicated that the user
experience is poor and this is linked to the
condition of the spaces and some of the
existing uses such as the commercial units
within the shopping center.

ACCESSIBLE AND INCLUSIVE DESIGN
The refurbishment and upgrade of the
Skenderija sports complex should achieve
recognized international standards and
best practice for accessibility and inclusive
design.
To support this goal, a detailed review
of accessibility and inclusive design
standards should be undertaken of all the
venues and the adjacent urban fabric and
transport links.
Accessible and inclusive design gives
people greater freedom and independence
of movement, and creates safer
environments, which greatly improve the
experience of all users, regardless of age,
impairment, gender, or other personal
circumstance.
The physical aspects of a design must not
create barriers that prevent access or limit
independence, dignity or choice for the
user. Provision of an inclusive environment
not only addresses the short-term
compliance with accessibility guidance, but
also the long-term implications of social
sustainability, attitude and viability.
The goal is to achieve consistency of
facilities provided for athletes, officials
and other professionals, staff, spectators
and the general public and set standards
of inclusivity and accessibility for all of
Sarajevo

THE HEART OF THE CENTER
To achieve an athlete-centerd environment, a
facility is proposed which we are calling the
Institute of Sport.
It will be the main reception of the center and
the focal point of the athletes’ and visitors’
arrival.
It is where the core principles of knowledge
sharing, nurturing, learning, inspiration,
aspiration and performance are connected
together for athletes and the wider public.
The Institute could centralize the following
facilities and services:
• Sports specific strength and conditioning
gym
• Sports science and sports medicine
• Physiotherapy
• Rehabilitation
• Research and analysis
The athletes’ daily training schedule will start
and finish at the Institute.
Each athlete will be physically and
physiologically performance tested,
monitored, analysed, treated, trained and
recover within the Institute.
Athletes’ individual bespoke performance
plans and analysis will be stored and
monitored in this building.
Nutrition and dining facilities should also be
provided for the athletes in the Institute.
The Institute building could also
accommodate offices for sports’ federations
and anti-doping stations.
