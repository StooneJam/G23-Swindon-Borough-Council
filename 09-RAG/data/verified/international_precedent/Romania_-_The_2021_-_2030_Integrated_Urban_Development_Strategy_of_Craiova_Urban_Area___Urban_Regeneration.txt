URBAN REGENERATION
THE IMPORTANCE OF INTEGRATED URBAN REGENERATION IN THE SUSTAINABLE
DEVELOPMENT OF CITIES
The public space is a defining component for the attractiveness of a city and the quality of life it
provides to its residents, representing the place where people can spend their free time, meet and
build the community spirit. The quality and accessibility of public space play an important role in the
quality of life of the city. The general term “public space” includes not only well-defined public spaces
such as parks, public squares or playgrounds, but also the streets or façades of the buildings, all of
which play an important role in the vitality and attractiveness of a city.
Public spaces fall into three main categories:
• Surface-like public spaces: large public spaces, with a cohesive role, which are a point of
attraction in the city, such as parks, public gardens or the civic center.
• Point-like public spaces: small in size (less than 3 ha), mainly of local interest, such as squares,
small squares or playgrounds for children, which take on the role of proximity facilities,
important for the quality of housing in neighborhoods.
• Linear-like public spaces: connecting elements between the attractions of a city, such as
pedestrian areas, boulevards, streets furnished with street furniture for leisure or socializing
activities.
Given that urban regeneration is a priority at European level and a direction of action that can be
financed from non-reimbursable external funds (the European Regional Development Fund and the
Recovery and Resilience Facility) with an estimated budget of 1.5 billion EUR, the public authorities
should take a proactive approach in identifying regeneration opportunities at the level of the cities
they represent or at the level of the metropolitan areas they belong to, in which case the projects can
be developed in partnership with the county councils.
By following an integrated approach, the public administration must establish the priority areas for
urban regeneration. Interventions on public spaces must be designed under a cohesive vision, in order
to avoid one-off interventions and to maximize the impact of investments on all hierarchical levels
(municipal, neighborhood, one-off), constantly aiming to improve the quality of life.
The complete list of spaces eligible for urban regeneration through projects financed from non-
reimbursable external funds, according to the emergency ordinance regulating these projects, are:
a) central areas;
b) historic and protected built areas;
c) public spaces within collective housing complexes, housing complexes or areas with
condominium-like housing, respectively;
d) areas of central and secondary train and bus stations, railway stations and port railway
stations located within or at the border of urban localities, whether they are in use,
abandoned, or have a different use;
e) cliffs, banks and islands of rivers, stream and lakes within the urban localities;
f) mine tailings and closed landfills within the urban localities;
g) unstructured peripheral residential areas, areas of degraded blocks of flats and informal
settlements in the urban environment;
h) disused industrial sites, areas with major disused infrastructures, disused military sites;
i) urban public gardens and parks, dendrological parks, botanical gardens;
j) urban agri-food markets.1

Regeneration interventions must be prioritized according to the different needs of the
neighborhoods, as resulting from their urban analysis. Craiova is a complex city, which faces the dual
problem of a declining quality of life in the neighborhoods of the urban core and an uncontrolled urban
expansion in the peri-urban ring, manifested through:
• the aging of the building stock and the problem of parking lots in the dense neighborhoods of
residential buildings
• the undeveloped infrastructure in
o the less developed single-family housing neighborhoods and marginalized areas
o the emerging peri-urban neighborhoods formed as a result of urban sprawl.
Therefore, the urban regeneration process should be guided both by the measures recommended
in Romania’s Urban Policy – the priority objectives of spatial sustainability and fair access to quality
public infrastructure and services, and by the specific needs and opportunities of each neighborhood.

The following preparatory tools can facilitate the process of prioritizing and developing projects,
while maximizing the impact of investments:
• Comprehensive and up-to-date inventory of green spaces, public spaces, heritage buildings
and public assets with functional reconversion potential (disused industrial sites, disused
military sites), for their preservation, regeneration and capitalization.
• Collaboration with other public or private entities, but also with neighboring administrations
or the county council, in order to help make use of strategic resources that are not under the
Municipality’s administration (e.g. The blue-green infrastructure, disused industrial or
military sites).
• Coordinating regeneration projects with other strategic objectives and investments, such as
coordinating the rehabilitation of a public space with mobility plans or stimulating private
investment in a priority area through investments in the quality of public spaces.
• Organizing urban planning and architecture design competitions to promote quality projects
and innovative solutions.
The degree of attractiveness and the impact of a space on the community depends on the quality of
the public space, its accessibility, image, functionality and degree of inclusiveness. Therefore, in
shaping a quality public space, a wide range of issues must be considered, beyond the architecture
and planning of the space, following four general levels: 1) access and connectivity, 2) proposed use
and activities, 3) comfort and image, 4) impact on the social environment.

THE RENOVATION WAVE – THE SUSTAINABLE REHABILITATION OF BUILDING
The age of the urban building stock is a challenge for the sustainable development of cities, a
challenge faced both at national level where 87% of urban housing was built before 1990 and at the
level of the European Union, where 85% of buildings were built more than 20 years ago and it is
expected that 85% -95 % of them will exist in 2050.
The European approach, detailed in the Renovation Wave and the European Green Deal, aims to
reduce energy consumption from fossil fuels for which the buildings are responsible, being one of the
main consumers of energy, being responsible for 40% of total energy consumption in the EU, while in
Romania, the household sector and the tertiary sector represent 45% of Romania's consumption.
• accelerating the thermal rehabilitation of the building stock, public and private, to achieve
the goal of energy performance, by energy modernization of existing buildings, both by
thermal insulation of the building envelope, as already practiced in Romania, and by
modernizing centralized systems for the supply of thermal and electrical utilities;
• increasing the number of buildings whose energy consumption is almost net zero (nZEB) for
new buildings, by using renewable energy sources and a high degree of energy efficiency.
The Renovation Wave will directly contribute to the preservation of buildings of cultural value and
to increase the attractiveness of existing housing and the quality of life, thus contributing to the
capitalization of built heritage, limiting uncontrolled urban expansion - suburbanization and reducing
energy consumption generated by the construction industry.
Following the goal of energy efficiency, an integrated approach to renovation is recommended, both
at the building level and at the neighborhood level, including seismic risk reduction, technical
expertise of the resistance structure and if needed, the necessary reinforcements, interventions to
make buildings more accessible for people with reduced mobility - especially in the context of aging
population, but also other architectural interventions such as transforming the roof of buildings into
public spaces or using them for the installation of solar panels, and the establishment of color and
image guides at the neighborhood level, in order to adapt them to the new housing standards. This
integrated approach is explored through the New European Bauhaus initiative, which encourages
solutions at the crossroads of innovation, culture, social inclusion, science and technology.
The objectives of The Renovation Wave both at European and national level, can only be achieved
through action at local level, aiming at:
• doubling the rate of renovation for energy efficiency at European level, as specified in the
strategy - "The Renovation Wave in Europe - Greening our buildings, creating jobs, improving
the quality of life" (2020);
• reduction of at least 30% of energy consumption for residential buildings and 30% of
greenhouse gas emissions for public buildings, of projects financed from the National
Recovery and Resilience Plan;
• targeting maximum efficiency thresholds and complying with European directives, national
legislation and technical specifications for energy efficiency and construction of nZEB
buildings, such as the National Long-Term Renovation Strategy (SRTL), nZEB Building Growth
Plan (2014), Law no. 372/2005 and Law no. 101/2020 regarding the amendment and
completion of law no. 372/2005, according to EU Directive 2018/844 on energy performance
and Directive 2012/27 on energy efficiency.
At the level of Craiova municipality, the state of the residential building stock, reveals the fact that
out of the 3172 collective housing blocks analyzed, only 778 (24.6%) were totally thermo-insulated,
902 (28.4%) are partially thermo-insulated and 1492 (47%) have not yet been thermo-insulated - see
fig. 20.

METHODOLOGY
This section briefly describes the process of analyzing public spaces for the purpose of urban
regeneration, outlining the steps needed to understand the local context and shape the main
directions of intervention. The analysis of public spaces and the urban regeneration strategy address
the issue of regeneration through an approach that is structured according to the urban scale of the
interventions:
• regeneration of representative public spaces, at municipal or metropolitan scale;
This category includes interventions aimed at revitalizing the historical areas, rehabilitating
and expanding the green spaces and making use of the potential to capitalize on mobility
corridors for use as public spaces.
• regeneration of residential neighborhoods, at the scale of neighborhood units and within
urban islands – building blocks;
This category includes in particular specific interventions for the regeneration of collective
housing compounds, those regarding connectivity and the functional and social mix in single
family neighborhoods or those regarding the expansion and modernization of the technical
municipal infrastructure and of the social and public transport connections in peri-urban
areas.
Urban regeneration aims to improve the physical environment, the vitality and socio-economic
dynamics of the urban environment by modernizing, rehabilitating or expanding the non-performing,
degraded, poorly used or abandoned spaces or buildings, in order to increase the attractiveness of the
city and the quality of urban life. When summarizing the literature and also the examples of best
practice in the field of urban regeneration, we can notice the following guiding principles:
• the principle of integrated urban regeneration
• the principle of connectivity and complete neighborhoods
• the principle of functional mix
• the principle of inclusiveness and social cohesion
• the principle of quality and authenticity in the rehabilitation of the public space
The regeneration of the public space must be a priority objective for both sprawling and shrinking
cities which face demographic, economic or spatial challenges, although the prioritization of actions
may differ depending on the typology of the city and the specificities of the local context. Urban
regeneration has a direct impact on the first three strategic objectives of Romania's Urban Policy:
• green and resilient cities, through interventions aimed at reducing the dependence on
vehicles and promoting non-motorized travel, but also through interventions aimed at
integrating, regenerating and capitalizing on the green spaces and the blue-green
infrastructure, to increase the citizens’ access to green spaces and to ensure a green and clean
environment;
• competitive and productive cities, by creating quality public spaces that stimulate the city's
attractiveness for housing, work and investment, but also by efficiently capitalizing on
buildings and land that are well located for strategic investments;
• just and inclusive cities, by increasing equitable access to public utilities, services and
facilities, by making the public domain accessible to all citizens – including the elderly or the
disabled, and by creating inclusive community spaces that foster a community spirit.

Addressing the regeneration of representative public spaces
The regeneration of representative public spaces requires the simultaneous analysis of the following
components and the integrated approach to their revitalization and interconnection:
• the green spaces and the elements of the natural setting;
• the central areas of the city/historic areas the protected built areas;
• the built heritage – cultural, architectural, industrial and public utility buildings;
• the main mobility corridors in the city.
The green spaces and natural environment resources were identified with the help of the Register of
Green Spaces developed at city level, where available, of the information received from the General
Directorate of the Public Domain, of the Google Maps platform, of the Corine Land Cover database
and of the European Environment Agency Natura 2000 database3. The analysis of resources focused
on the following assessment criteria:
• quantification of the area of the green spaces, in terms of per capita area, compared to the
standard of 26 m2 regulated at national and European level – this step is the first level of
analysis, helping us identify opportunities and challenges in ensuring equitable access to
public space;
• identification of the green spaces that are accessible to the general public, such as parks and
public gardens – an important step, given that many of the areas quantified in the previous
step are not set up as actual public spaces or do not have a satisfactory quality;
The main mobility corridors in the city were identified both by the empirical analysis of the urban
fabric structure and by the “scientific” analysis, from a spatial (geometric) point of view, of the degree
of accessibility of the street grid, the latter analysis being performed by Space Syntax Romania using
their own methodological models. They are identified by analyzing the road system at a “global” level
– i.e. the likelihood of a segment to be transited when using the shortest path between any two points
in the system. These highly accessible streets coincide with the large arterial roads, but beyond the
intensity of car traffic, these arterial roads open certain opportunities:
• they are conducive to economic activities, due to the high degree of accessibility and
pedestrian traffic generated, but for this they must meet certain quality criteria, which make
them conducive to pedestrian mobility and promenade activities;
• the street profile allows their reconfiguration as green corridors that connect and integrate
the main green spaces, the elements of the natural environment or the attractions of
municipal importance.
In addition, the main public spaces in the central area were analyzed – surface-like spaces (e.g. parks,
civic markets), linear public spaces (e.g. pedestrian area, connecting elements), point-like spaces (e.g.
squares) and the cultural, architectural and industrial built heritage, identified in the list of historical
monuments drawn up by the Ministry of Culture, but also other objectives of local interest, which are
not formally included in this list.
In order to prioritize and detail the urban regeneration measures, these spaces must be analyzed
from a qualitative point of view, by assessing a few key aspects:
• the connectivity to the main pedestrian, public and non-motorized transport networks;
• the representativeness of the public space and the human scale of the urban elements;
• the existence and quality of street furniture, street lighting, shading, etc.;
• the accessibility for people with reduced mobility in the public space and its contribution to
social cohesion.

Addressing neighborhood regeneration
The aging building stock and the increasing dependence on cars have had a negative impact on the
quality of life in residential neighborhoods. In the neighborhoods, there are two general issues:
• the limited access to neighborhood facilities and public spaces in the vicinity of homes,
according to the principle of pedestrian accessibility;
• the prioritization of car traffic over non-motorized travel and the parking lots that suffocate
the public domain, issues that affect the quality of the public space and pedestrian mobility.
At the same time, the challenges and opportunities vary according to the typology of the
neighborhoods, requiring a differentiated approach for:
1. the collective housing complexes from the socialist period, comprising 69.7% of the urban
dwellings, where the regeneration of the apartment blocks, the solving of the parking
problems and a better access to endowments and quality public space must be prioritized.
2. the areas of single-family dwellings within the urban core, with a dense urban fabric, where
priority should be given to facilitating pedestrian accessibility and increasing the access to
neighborhood facilities.
3. the rural settlements under the city’s administration where access to basic public services
and connection to the urban core must be ensured.
Marginalized areas and informal settlements require an approach adapted to the living conditions of
the identified communities, both through investments in infrastructure – from providing basic public
services at affordable prices, to waste management and regeneration of the built stock or the
construction of new social housing, and also through community development and social integration
programs – retraining programs, social assistance, access to education.4
The neighborhood regeneration approach should aim at analyzing the built stock and identifying
revitalization opportunities in a structured manner, according to the following principles:
• The principle of the integrated approach to green spaces, which aims to capitalize on all
green spaces in the neighborhood, from green pockets to parks, and to interconnect them,
by consolidating green corridors along the main mobility axes, which will also define the
boundaries of the neighborhoods.
• The principle of functional mix and of the complementarity of neighborhood facilities, which
aims to shape complete 15-20 minute neighborhoods, an urban planning concept that aims
to facilitate access to a complete set of facilities close to home – public transport, school,
kindergarten, sports field, playground, social spaces, convenience store, etc. This principle
guides the prioritization of urban regeneration interventions in a neighborhood, aiming to
obtain a complementarity of the activities and services offered.
• The principle of neighborhood units, which aims to facilitate pedestrian accessibility of all
residents to quality services and public spaces, by structuring interventions around
neighborhood units, which follow the principle of pedestrian accessibility within a radius of
400 m (5 minutes), the optimal distance that can be comfortably walked on foot5. After
implementing this principle, the interventions can go even further, by densifying the facilities
around local communities, with a servicing radius of approximately 200 m (2.5 minutes).
• The principle of creating a hierarchy of the public space, aiming at the configuration of urban
islands, within which to reorganize the parking spaces and structure the open space according
to the degree of accessibility, outlining both public and private spaces.

Regeneration of green spaces and capitalization of the natural environment
Green spaces are important not only for their aesthetic and recreational role, but also for the impact
that green areas play in creating a healthy and pleasant environment for people. At the same time,
green spaces can contribute to improving air quality, fighting the urban heat island effect, reducing
urban noise or managing flood risks.
Access to quality green spaces directly influences the quality of life in the city, the well-being and
health of the residents, so preserving and expanding green areas and facilitating the access of all
citizens to green public spaces in the vicinity of housing must be a priority in urban development,
despite possible policies for densification of certain urban areas.
At the same time, the blue-green infrastructure is an important resource for increasing the quality
of life in cities, and municipalities should pay more attention to the smart use of these resources, both
for recreational purposes and in adapting to climate change and combating urban pollution. In cities
or at the peri-urban ring level there are often important vegetation resources, NATURA 2000 sites or
lakes, which should be seen as a whole as an urban ecosystem that can make up for the limited green
space inside dense neighborhoods or the lack thereof in new peri-urban neighborhoods.
According to legislative provisions at national and European level, cities must have a minimum of
26 m2 of green space per capita, a threshold that 73.4% of cities in Romania do not comply with.
However, even for those who meet this minimum criterion, it is important to also assess the quality
of these spaces in terms of accessibility and facilities. In expanding the network of green spaces or
regenerating existing spaces, it is important to follow the principle of fair accessibility, facilitating
pedestrian access of all residents to a green space – Romania's Urban Policy recommends setting a
standard of 15 m2/capita within a 10 minutes’ walk radius (800 m).

Regeneration of linear spaces – the main mobility corridors
Linear public spaces include both spaces dedicated to promenade and leisure activities, as well as
representative pedestrian areas, the developed banks of the rivers or the portions of the boulevards
provided with urban furniture, as well as all the streets of the city, which are essentially public spaces,
even if too few are capitalized.
The streets have the potential to be reconfigured so that they stimulate economic activities, facilitate
a safe and pleasant environment for walking or other non-motorized travel, to be green corridors and
even function as a public space themselves, by implementing sharing policies or by setting up street
furniture on the sidewalks. Urban regeneration measures for these areas should simultaneously
pursue the following directions of action:
• improve the quality of the representative line-like public spaces, such as pedestrian alleys,
as an integral part of the regeneration of the central areas or green corridors;
• regenerate the main mobility corridors of municipal importance, improve connectivity in the
urban environment and change the paradigm of prioritizing modes of travel, from car to
pedestrian and non-motorized means: pedestrian> cyclist> public transport> personal car;
• reconfigure the streets in the residential neighborhoods, facilitate pedestrian movements,
share the streets and revitalize the façades and activities on the ground floor of the blocks of
flats – the measures will be elaborated in the chapter dedicated to the integrated
regeneration of the neighborhoods.
At the level of Romanian cities, there are four street categories, depending on traffic intensity and
on the role, they have in the structure of the street layout and the city. Each category presents
different opportunities and limitations for urban regeneration, but a dominant feature of these, at
present, is the fact that measures and interventions are prioritized towards the comfort of the vehicle,
to the detriment of pedestrians and other means of transport.
• Category I streets – main roads:
• Category II streets – connecting streets:
• Category III streets – collector streets:
• Category IV streets – for local use:
Category I and II streets play an important role in the connectivity of the city, taking over the major
flows of the city and ensuring the connection between the functional and residential areas. They often
coincide with important arteries for the city’s economic activity and for connecting the main public
spaces or cultural objectives, thus requiring special attention to the safety and comfort of pedestrians
or cyclists. A street that is carefully configured for the human scale, with vegetation, adequate public
lighting and urban furniture, will have a positive impact on economic activities and will become a space
for walking and socializing.
The economic dimension and the social dimension are mutually reinforcing, but both are
conditioned by the quality of the public space setup – a street with shops, but unfriendly to
pedestrians, or one carefully designed for pedestrians but lacking a functional mix will not generate
the necessary pedestrian flow for economic activities to flourish. A safe street, with spaces for rest
and socialization and economic diversity, will always be attractive and will have great economic
potential.
A paradigm shift is needed in prioritizing the means of transport, through an approach focused
primarily on reducing dependence on vehicles and facilitating public transport and non-motorized
travel, in order to achieve the goals of sustainability and resilience and build a green city with quality
public spaces.

Regeneration of the central area and of the protected built areas
The historical areas, the protected ones and the civic centers of cities have an important role in
strengthening the local identity through their authenticity and architectural richness, being elements
that must be protected and highlighted. The main representative public spaces of the city, such as
public squares and pedestrian streets, are usually also found in the central area, thus concentrating
economic and recreational activities that give dynamism to public life.
The quality of public spaces is essential to the attractiveness of the central area, both for leisure
activities aimed at locals and tourists. The quality of the buildings, public spaces and the easy
connections between them are important in capitalizing on historical areas and cultural specificity for
tourism purposes, but also in creating an attractive environment for investors. In order to protect the
specificity of the place, it is necessary to regulate and implement measures to ensure compliance with
the architectural context in future interventions and constructions. Jan Gehl identifies the following
essential elements of a quality public space, that should energize public life9:
• Protection
o Protection against vehicular traffic
o Protection of the public space against crime and violence (crime prevention)
o Protection against unpleasant sensory experiences (rain, wind, heat)
• Comfort
o Invitations for walking
o Invitations for standing and staying
o Invitations for sitting
o Invitations for seeing
o Invitations for hearing & talking
o Invitations for play and recreation
• Quality of the public space/Enjoyment
o Dimensioned at a human scale
o Positive aspects of climate (sun, shade, etc.)
o Aesthetic quality
The traditional squares of the historic and compact areas of a city, defined by the surrounding
buildings, are usually among the highest quality public spaces. The quality of a square is also
dependent on its proportions (for example, the ratio between the area and the height of the
surrounding buildings). Often, public spaces (re)designed in the socialist period were not conceived at
a human scale, but as large open spaces, creating a hostile environment for recreational activities.
Small structures, urban furniture and trees can create a pleasant transition from the street level to the
height of the surrounding buildings. At the same time, there should be continuous pedestrian
connectivity within and between the public spaces, because barriers to the natural movement of the
pedestrians fragment the public space, reduce the dynamism of isolated spaces and have a negative
impact on the perception of public space quality.
The old town of Craiova, recently rehabilitated through a EU-funded project in the form of a
pedestrian area, concentrates numerous cultural institutions, museums, administrative buildings,
churches, but also a series of houses with architectural value, which are on the list of historical
monuments. The area of Craiova's old town is delimited perimetrally by C.S. Nicolaescu Str .– Plopșor
Str., Alexandru Ioan Cuza Str., Arieș Str., C.D. Fortunescu Str. and Mihail Kogălniceanu Str., the streets
inside being mainly intended to be used for pedestrian traffic, highlighting the buildings in the area.
Mihai Viteazu Square - City Hall. The main promenade and leisure relaxation area is also located here,
because, after the restoration of the old town, numerous shops, restaurants, terraces and cake shops
appeared here, activating and concentrating the public life.

Regeneration of the built heritage
The built heritage plays an important role in strengthening local identity and authenticity, both
cultural heritage and protected historical monuments, as well as other buildings representative of the
local community or the history of the city, whether they are part of a city's industrial heritage or
buildings hosting public functions of municipal interest such as railway stations, bus stations or agri-
food markets. Their careful analysis in the urban context is necessary for the process of prioritizing
urban regeneration projects, and it must follow these directions:
• Identifying vulnerable heritage sites and buildings that need rehabilitation, modernization
or additional urban regulations in the area, in order to protect their architectural value,
increase their functionality and better integrate them into the urban fabric;
• spatializing and connecting cultural objectives through pedestrian corridors, bicycles or
public transport routes, in order to unify the way they are presented and crystallize the
cultural identity of the city, thus having a direct impact on tourist attractiveness;
• the functional reconversion of abandoned heritage buildings, aiming to capitalize on them
for the benefit of the local communities (e.g. neighborhood centers) and accelerating the
regeneration process, by establishing public-private partnerships with investors interested in
rehabilitating and carrying out economic/civic activities or by other fiscal instruments to
stimulate the rehabilitation of privately owned valuable buildings.
Craiova City has a rich architectural and cultural heritage, built due to its position as a key city in
national history, the center of great historical decisions since the 15th century, Craiova being
considered in the past the second capital of the country or the capital of the Romanian aristocracy.
The cultural profile of Craiova was marked by the personalities of the city who contributed to the
establishment of the first educational institutions in Wallachia, but also by boyars, aristocrats and
rulers who attracted architects and artists from the country, but also from France, Germany or Italy,
who offered the city numerous buildings with a special architecture, among which buildings belonging
to the Brâncovenesc, Renaissance, Baroque, Classical, Neoclassical or Romantic style stand out.
Although the city was deeply transformed during the communist period, many heritage buildings were
saved from the demolitions of this period, positioning Craiova on a leading place among the Romanian
cities that can be proud of their rich architectural heritage.
According to the data provided by the Ministry of Culture, Craiova has a significant number of
monumental buildings in the country, 310 in Craiova City and 7 in its dependent localities - 1 in
Rovine, 4 in Mofleni, 1 in Popoveni, 1 in Șimnicu de Sus, among them 15 monuments of national or
universal interest, representing a percentage of 5% of the total, while the other 305 (95%) are of local
interest. Those of national interest are:
• The ensemble of the Craiova Archdiocese and the Metropolitan Church of Oltenia
• The Assumption of the Virgin Mary Church (Madona Dudu)
• All Saints Wooden Church
• Băniei houses
• Glogoveanu House (Dolj Court)
• Nicolae Romanescu House

INTEGRATED URBAN REGENERATION OF NEIGHBORHOODS
Analysis of the attractiveness of the neighborhoods in Craiova and its peri-urban area
The structure of the neighborhoods in Craiova City and its peri-urban area can be divided into:
1) The central zone, which concentrates the main cultural, historical and representative public
spaces of the city, around which there are both low-rise housing units as well as collective
housing buildings dating from the socialist period.
2) Collective housing neighborhoods - George Enescu, Craiovița Nouă, Brazda lui Novac, Piața
Gării, Rovine, Sărari, 1 Mai, Lăpuș Argeș, Valea Roșie, which are usually well served by public
facilities, but the quality of life is affected by the overcrowding of the public space by parked
cars and the insufficient number of spaces for recreation and socialization in the vicinity of the
house.
3) (Old) low-rise urban neighborhoods - Știrbei Vodă, Brestei, Siloz, Ungureni, Cernele, Craiovița
Veche, Nisipuri Dorobănția, Severinului, Fața Luncii, Lascăr Catargiu, Mofleni, Popoveni,
Romanești, Dunării, with a very dense urban fabric and where the quality of housing and the
degree of neighborhood facilities coverage varies. Here and there, the quality of transport /
pedestrian / technical-urban / social infrastructure is poor, making these neighborhoods
difficult to cross on foot. While some neighborhoods are close to the central area or are well
connected to the public transport network, others are located at a significant distance from
the center and are difficult to reach without a car.
4) Newly developed neighborhoods on the outskirts of the city, both within the territorial
administrative limits - the Veteranilor, Hipodrom, Bordei, Plaiul Vulcănești, Bariera Vâlcii
neighborhoods, as well as in the localities in the south and east of the peri-urban ring - Valea
Fetii, Preajba Nouă, Banu Mărăcine. These neighborhoods usually consist of single-family
homes or low height regime buildings, have a high degree of fragmentation in the
development of plots and have deficiencies in terms of technical-urban and transport
infrastructure - both unpaved streets, missing sidewalks and lack of metropolitan public
transport lines, basic social infrastructure - schools, health care facilities, but also in terms of
the access to public spaces and neighborhood facilities - playgrounds, socializing areas,
proximity stores.
5) Dependent villages - Făcăi, Mofleni, Popoveni, Șimnicu de Jos, Cernele, Cernele de Sus,
Rovine, Izvoru Rece, small settlements, located at the border between the TAU and the peri-
urban localities. The main objectives for these areas are to facilitate fair access to the basic -
technical-urban and social infrastructure and a more efficient connection with the central
area, to connect the inhabitants to the main attractions, be they employers, commercial
spaces, cultural and leisure areas, etc.
The complex structure of Craiova City requires urban regeneration programs adapted to each type
of neighborhood, and the implementation must be done in an integrated way both at the level of the
neighborhood or neighborhood unit, and at the level of the development directions, by coordinating
regeneration projects with those covering mobility and infrastructure. An integrated and adapted
approach will increase the attractiveness of both old and newly developed neighborhoods, thus
reducing unjustified land consumption and the uncontrolled urban expansion.
In order to reduce dependence on the car in the pursuit of the goal of achieving green and resilient
cities, it is important to analyze the location of housing areas relative to the main mobility corridors
which connect them to other functional areas of the city. The prioritization process must aim at
ensuring fair access to public transport by expanding the transport network, streamlining the existing
transport lines and increasing the frequency, so that public transport becomes a reliable means of
transport. At the same time, pedestrian accessibility must be improved, through infrastructure
interventions in poor areas and the management of parking spaces in dense neighborhoods of
collective housing, so that travel is safe and comfortable.

Access to neighborhood facilities in the vicinity of homes contributes to increasing the quality of life
in neighborhoods, and urban regeneration of neighborhoods following the principles of
complementarity and neighborhood units will help reduce car dependence by providing the necessary
facilities for daily life at a comfortable walking distance. For the new neighborhoods in peri-urban
areas, which often do not have the necessary infrastructure, a priority is to expand and improve the
quality of streets, sidewalks and to build schools and neighborhood facilities.
Integrated rehabilitation of apartment buildings from the socialist period in terms of the building,
the neighborhood and the levels of intervention (energy efficiency, architecture and structure) will
contribute the quality of public space and housing, making these neighborhoods and buildings
competitive options for housing. Particular attention must be paid to the accessibility of residential
buildings, both to ensure easy access for people with disabilities, those with strollers and the elderly,
encouraging a more active lifestyle and involvement in the community.

Regeneration opportunities in collective housing neighborhoods
Regeneration actions must aim to reduce the dominance of the car and to prioritize non-motorized
travel, the common challenge of collective housing neighborhoods being the suffocation of public
space by parked cars and the lack of attention to pedestrian comfort and community spaces. Large
collective housing compounds are complex neighborhoods which require an integrated approach,
which can be most effectively achieved by developing master plans at the neighborhood level, to
develop diagnostic analysis and identify key challenges and opportunities.
• Complex interventions that require coordination plans - reconfiguring parking lots and open
spaces around urban islands. The degree of permeability of the urban fabric and implicitly the
size of the urban islands influence the quality of the public space - a permeable area, with
small urban islands, creating an environment conducive to pedestrian traffic. In Craiova City,
the permeability degree is higher in the central area and in the apartment building
neighborhoods, while the areas of new or old houses have a lower degree of permeability.
Possible interventions around urban islands include: reconfiguring parking spaces to their
outer limits and in multi-story parking lots to recover community spaces, capitalizing on green
spaces and open spaces inside the island, reconfiguring local streets as shared spaces,
allocating complementary functions to the recovered spaces to serve the local community or
to form neighborhood sub-centers, rehabilitation of playgrounds and existing urban furniture.
• Common lines of action to bring specific improvements of the quality of life in
neighborhoods, which can become local government programs:
o developing schoolyards so that they serve the local community outside of school
hours, as playgrounds or sports facilities.
o capitalization of streets as line-like public spaces:
- streets with a high degree of accessibility - capitalizing on the economic and
community potential,
- local service streets - reconfiguration as shared public spaces.
o capitalizing on open spaces and their repurposing with public facilities - playgrounds,
sports fields, socializing spaces for various interests, etc.
- nodes with a high degree of accessibility (e.g. squares),
- green, inaccessible spaces with spontaneous vegetation, around apartment
buildings,
- vacant lots or other spaces recovered from the reconfiguration of parking lots.
